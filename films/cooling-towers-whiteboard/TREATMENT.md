# Where does the heat go? (Whiteboard Explainer)

How cooling towers are used in data centres, explained on one glossy whiteboard in about 95 s, with English narration.

## 1. Three candidate structures

1. **The journey of one watt.** A red magnet (the "heat") drops onto a tiny chip. The pen draws its route across the board as it goes: chip → air → chilled-water loop → chiller → condenser loop → cooling tower → sky. The camera rides the line. At the tower it pushes inside, then whips to the catch (water), and finally pulls back to show the whole route as one picture.
2. **A proof.** The claim "evaporating 1% of the water cools the rest by 6 °C" goes at the top, and the board fills downward step by step (latent heat, the wet bulb, energy saved), each step boxed. It's clear, but the system itself (loops, chiller, tower) gets lost, and that system is what people actually want to understand.
3. **Before / after duet.** Two pens draw two data halls side by side: one air-cooled, with its thermometer climbing, and one with a tower. It's good for the efficiency comparison, but it splits attention and loses the single path the heat takes.

**Chosen: 1, the journey of one watt.** The topic *is* a path, and the whiteboard's native moves fit it exactly: the pen leads the camera along a pipeline, and a magnet is the only thing allowed to move. The heat becomes a character with a goal (get out of the building), and the turn is the moment it leaves: the magnet **lifts off the board** as vapour.

- **Opening image:** an empty board. An orange magnet drops onto it with a clack, and the pen writes "1 watt" beside it. The hero is on screen from frame 1.
- **Signature shot:** at the tower, the camera rides the condenser pipe up and into the fan. The music cuts to a near-silence, the magnet lifts off the board (its shadow grows, it shrinks into vapour puffs) and the camera follows it up off the top of the tower.
- **Ending:** the eraser wipes the board clean except for one surviving line, the route from chip to sky. The pen writes "chip → water → sky" under it and caps. No pull-back to a wall.
- **Score shape:** a synth arpeggio (it's a tech topic) whose register climbs station by station as the heat climbs toward the roof. Kalimba joins whenever water appears. There's a near-silence for the lift-off, then a thinner, lower reprise for the water cost, ending on a warm sustained chord under the wipe.

Compared with the library demo, all six aspects differ: structure (a single route with a hero, not mechanism → catch → duet → rewind), opening (a magnet drop, not a tiny object pull-back), signature shot (the lift-off, not a full-board pull-back), camera path (one continuous track, not returns and a wall reveal), score (a synth arpeggio and kalimba, not clockwork marimba) and ending (a wipe to one line, not an end card under the title).

## 2. Logline and arc

A watt of heat from your last chatbot question has to get out of a data centre; we follow it through two water loops to a cooling tower, where a little water evaporates and carries it into the sky.

- **Setup:** a magnet becomes "1 watt"; servers turn almost all their power into heat (a 100 kW+ AI rack).
- **Journey:** air → cooling unit → chilled water → chiller (which can switch off on cold days) → condenser water → roof.
- **Turn:** inside the tower, about 1% of the water evaporates and the rest comes out about 6 °C cooler, like sweat. The magnet lifts off: the heat has left.
- **Cost:** what it leaves behind is a water bill: millions of litres a day at a big site, tracked as WUE (litres per kWh), and increasingly recycled water.
- **Ending:** the board is wiped except for the route: chip → water → sky.

## 3. Benchmark

- **Learn from** the hand-drawn engineering explainers of the 2010s (the RSA-style continuous board) and from good process animation in plant-tour films. Learn one continuous board, the camera travelling instead of cutting, each station of a process getting its own region, and the camera holding still on the one number that matters.
- **Don't take** their characters, hands, drawings or music. We use no hand at all, and no drawn mascots.

## 6. Cue map

- 92 bpm (beat 0.652 s), 4/4, in E-flat major with a C minor reprise.
- **Station 0 (rack):** a soft sine pad and a low arpeggio in 8ths (octave 3).
- **Each new station** raises the arpeggio's register by a step, and its filter opens a little: octave 3 → 3.5 → 4 → 4.5 at the tower. Kalimba plinks enter on the blue loop (water) and play whenever water is on screen.
- **Tower:** the full brightness, a light pulse on the beat.
- **Lift-off:** a near-silence (the pad only, falling to nothing), then a rising glassy chime as the magnet leaves.
- **Water notes:** C minor, the arpeggio low and filtered, the kalimba alone on top.
- **Wipe:** a sustained warm E-flat chord swells under the eraser and rings out over the cap click.
- The voice comes first. The magnet drop, the lift-off and the cap click are fixed hits that the music sits around.

Mix: the voice on top and compressed, the music about 10 dB under it and ducked under speech, and the foley about 11 dB under the voice. Target −14 LUFS. ffmpeg on this machine is 6.x, so loudness is normalised in Python (two-pass) and ffmpeg only muxes.

## 8. Subtitles and titles

- All board text is in a single-line handwriting font written stroke by stroke, in three marker colours with fixed meanings:
  - **Black** `#23262c`: things and structure.
  - **Blue** `#2a5cb3`: water and process.
  - **Orange** `#d97757`: heat, the one accent.
- The title, "Where does the heat go?", is written on the board right after the hook. The end line, "chip → water → sky", is written in the same hand under the surviving route.
- Subtitles are burned in: an off-white rounded label with a handwriting-like face of about 44 px, dark text and a short orange dash. They are split at clauses, use at most 44 characters per line, keep the bottom 12 % of the frame free, and are also exported as `subtitles_en.srt`.
- Voice: Kokoro, a warm British narrator (`bm_george` or similar). Numbers are spelled out in the TTS text and written as digits on the board.
